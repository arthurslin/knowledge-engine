## The idea

Political scientists have long asked whether voters judge incumbents fairly. One answer, called retrospective voting, is that voters look back at how things went and reward or punish whoever was in charge. The worry is that voters cannot easily separate what leaders caused from what simply happened to them.

The most famous illustration comes from Christopher Achen and Larry Bartels, who set it out most fully in their 2016 book *Democracy for Realists*. In the summer of 1916, four people were killed by sharks along the New Jersey shore, in Monmouth and Ocean counties. Resorts lost business during the summer season. That November, President Woodrow Wilson ran for reelection. Achen and Bartels compared county results with Wilson's 1912 vote and reported that he did about 3 percentage points worse in the beach counties, and roughly 10 points worse in some Ocean County shore towns. Their reading: voters punished a president for sharks.

A related study pointed the same way. Andrew Healy, Neil Malhotra and Cecilia Hyunjung Mo, writing in PNAS in 2010, reported that a home college football win in the 10 days before an election gave the incumbent party about 1.61 extra percentage points. In a survey during the 2009 NCAA basketball tournament, they found that wins lifted approval of President Obama, but the effect vanished when respondents were first reminded of the scores.

## Why it might change a decision

The dispute that followed is a working example of a check anyone can apply to a surprising observational result: ask whether the effect shows up where the story says it should be strongest, and stays away from where it should be absent. A reader evaluating a claim that some irrelevant event swayed voters, consumers or patients can look for that pattern, and treat a finding that passes only its headline test as provisional.

## How solid is this?

Both original findings come from observational data, and the shark result rests on a single election in a single state. That is the crux of the dispute.

In 2018 Anthony Fowler and Andrew Hall published a reassessment in the *Journal of Politics*. They first reproduced the New Jersey estimate, then varied reasonable choices: which counties count as beach counties, whether to include Essex County, how to control for party machines. In their working-paper version, most of nearly 400 such specifications gave smaller estimates, and about three quarters were not statistically significant. At the town level, they report that the large drop shrinks after correcting what they describe as data errors, such as the handling of towns whose boundaries changed, and that it does not appear in other beach counties.

They then assembled every fatal shark attack in US history and county returns for presidential elections from 1872 to 2012. They found no systematic evidence that attacks hurt incumbents; the working-paper estimate was about half a point, indistinguishable from zero. In placebo tests on state elections with no attacks, estimates as large as New Jersey's turned up fairly often, which they read as a sign that the 1916 result could arise by chance.

Achen and Bartels replied in the same issue. They argue that 1916 was unusual: the attacks caused real economic losses, drew wide publicity and were, they argue, blamed on the national government, while most later attacks were isolated events nobody blamed on the president. They defend excluding Essex County on political grounds and dispute how one Ocean County town should be coded, and say a drop of around 3 points survives sensible specifications. In their view, finding no effect across other attacks does not show that this one had no effect.

The football finding followed a similar path. In PNAS in 2015, Fowler and Pablo Montagnes reproduced the original estimate but reported that it was no larger in counties with more interest in football, appeared outside the team's home county, did not shrink when no incumbent was on the ballot, and had no counterpart in NFL games. They concluded it was likely a false positive. Healy, Malhotra and Mo responded that the auxiliary tests were poorly designed, and that the betting-spread analysis and the survey experiment still pointed to a real effect.

The strongest competing explanation for both original results is that flexible analyses of a few unusual events will sometimes produce striking estimates by chance. The strongest case for them is that the specific mechanism, voters misattributing their mood or losses, has some independent support. The popular version, "sharks swung an election", is a lot stronger than either side's evidence supports. Neither dispute has a consensus winner.
