## What happened

The Seventh Review Conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons met in Geneva from 16 November 2026 and, after talks ran past the scheduled close, finished in the early hours of Saturday 21 November. The Convention has 128 states parties and takes decisions by consensus. One of the main items was the future of its Group of Governmental Experts on lethal autonomous weapons systems, which has discussed the issue for more than a decade.

A proposal for the group's mandate, published by the UN Office for Disarmament Affairs, said the group was "to negotiate a new Protocol to the Convention". It would meet 15 days a year in Geneva and report a draft protocol to the 2028 meeting of states parties. Protocols to this Convention are binding on the states that join them.

That wording did not survive. According to the Asia-Pacific Institute for Law and Security, states agreed that the group will elaborate, by consensus, an instrument on these weapons without prejudging its nature, building on a set of elements adopted in September 2026. It will meet in 2027 and 2028, and its 2028 report is to recommend the instrument's legal form, including whether it should be a binding protocol. At the closing meeting, the president recorded the adoption of the decisions and set 2027 sessions for 1 to 5 March and 30 August to 3 September.

The conference did not complete its main formal task. The president told the closing session that states were unable to agree on procedural matters, so the substantive review of the Convention did not take place. APILS reports that Russia blocked consensus on the agenda and rules of procedure after a dispute over the participation of observers such as the European Union and civil society groups.

## What is claimed vs. what is verified

Verified from UN material: the chair's proposed mandate text, and the closing meeting's record of adopted decisions, 2027 dates and the failure to agree procedure. The closing record is an automatic transcript that the UN says is not an official record. The final adopted decision text had not been published on the UN disarmament document site when this post was written, so the adopted wording here comes from APILS and campaign reporting rather than the official document.

Positions of individual states come from reporting. Reuters, in a report published by Al-Monitor at the start of the week, said the United States favours guidelines over binding rules and had proposed a five-year mandate for talks towards a non-binding agreement, and that the United States, Russia and Israel argue existing humanitarian law is sufficient. UK Stop Killer Robots, a campaign group, wrote that diplomats also named the UK, South Korea, Japan, Turkey and Israel as resisting binding rules, and that more than 95 states supported a legally binding instrument during the conference. Those characterisations come from a group that campaigns for a treaty and have not been confirmed against government statements.

## Why it matters

The Convention is the main standing forum where countries that develop military AI, including the United States and Russia, discuss limits on weapons that select and attack targets without a person deciding each strike. The outcome sets the pace there for two more years. A mandate to negotiate a protocol would have pointed towards binding law in 2028; the adopted mandate leaves that question to the 2028 report, which itself requires consensus. Separately, DroneXL reports that 163 states voted in October in the UN General Assembly's First Committee for a resolution calling for negotiations, with five against, so the issue is also being pursued outside the consensus forum.

## Open questions and other views

Campaigners describe the result as a failure. Stop Killer Robots' executive director, Nicole van Rooijen, said delay "puts civilians and humanity at risk". Human Rights Watch called the outcome disappointing.

States resisting a treaty argue, as Reuters reported, that the existing laws of war already govern these weapons and that voluntary guidance can adapt faster than a treaty. Russia's foreign ministry, in remarks reported by AP and cited by DroneXL, has said talk of restrictions or bans is premature.

APILS, a research institute that supports a protocol, called the new mandate a basis to build on, while noting that a clear mandate to negotiate a binding instrument proved impossible. Open questions include whether the consensus rule will let any single state block the 2028 recommendation, and whether talks among states that want a treaty will move to another forum.
