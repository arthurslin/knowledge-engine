## The idea

Ancient economies left few numbers behind. There are no Roman output statistics, and historians argue over whether the empire's prosperity was broad or confined to a thin elite. One unexpected record comes from Greenland. Silver, the metal of Roman coinage, was mostly extracted from lead-bearing ores, and smelting those ores released lead into the air. Some of it travelled thousands of kilometres north and fell with snow onto the Greenland ice sheet, where each year's snow became a thin layer of ice. Drill an ice core, measure lead layer by layer, and you get something like an annual chart of Mediterranean mining.

The idea is older than it sounds. In 1994, Sungmin Hong, Jean-Pierre Candelone, Clair Patterson and Claude Boutron reported in *Science* that Greenland ice from roughly 500 BCE to 300 CE held lead at about four times natural levels, which they attributed to Greek and Roman mining and smelting. They estimated that cumulative fallout over those eight centuries reached up to 15% of the fallout later caused by leaded petrol. That study rested on a small number of samples.

In 2018, Joe McConnell and colleagues, writing in *PNAS*, rebuilt the record in far greater detail: more than 21,000 measurements covering 1100 BCE to 800 CE, according to the Desert Research Institute's release, combined with atmospheric modelling that traced most of the lead to the Iberian Peninsula. The result is a curve that moves with known history. Emissions rose with Phoenician trade, dipped during the First Punic War, climbed as Carthage and then Rome expanded silver mining, and reached a sustained high during the first two centuries of the Roman Empire. Then, in the second century, they dropped, coinciding with the Antonine Plague that began around 165 CE, and stayed low for more than 500 years.

A second line of evidence comes from the Alps. In 2017, Alexander More and colleagues analysed a core from the Colle Gnifetti glacier with laser-based methods fine enough to resolve individual years. In about 2,000 years of record, they found only one stretch when lead fell to below detection limits: roughly 1349 to 1353, the years of the Black Death, when written records indicate that mining upwind of the site had stopped.

## Why it might change a decision

The finding gives historians a dated, physical series against which to test claims that otherwise rest on scattered texts. A concrete, testable consequence: if a historian argues that a particular crisis, such as a third-century political collapse, ruined the Roman monetary economy, the claim predicts a visible fall in lead at that time in independently dated cores. If the cores show no change, the claim needs another mechanism. The 2018 authors note one such cross-check already: silver content in Roman coinage declined in parallel with the lead record.

The Black Death result also changes a number used in modern policy. Pollution studies often treat "pre-industrial" lead as natural background. The Alpine core suggests that true natural levels were about an order of magnitude lower than values previously treated as low, because people had been polluting the air almost continuously for two millennia.

## How solid is this?

These are observational records, not experiments, and each major claim rests on a small number of cores. The 2018 reconstruction comes mainly from one Greenland site, supported by peat-bog records cited by the authors; the Black Death minimum comes from one Alpine glacier. Dating is a strength rather than a weakness: both records use annual layers, and the Alpine one was checked with radiocarbon dating. The detection of Roman-era pollution itself has been replicated, from the 1994 study through the 2018 one.

The weaker link is interpretation. Lead measures smelting, mostly in particular mining districts, not farming, trade or living standards, which made up most of the ancient economy. A fall in lead could mean fewer miners, exhausted ores, or a shift to other silver sources, as well as a general depression. Wind patterns and transport to Greenland also vary. The 2018 paper's link between the Antonine Plague and the long low is a timing match, and the plague's death toll is itself contested among historians.

The popular headline that ice cores "measure the Roman economy" outruns the data somewhat. A more careful summary is that they measure one industry, closely tied to coinage, with unusual precision.
