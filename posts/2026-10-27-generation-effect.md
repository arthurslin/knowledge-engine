## The idea

In 1978 the psychologists Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf published five experiments in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory* comparing two ways of meeting the same word. In the read condition a participant saw a pair printed in full, such as "rapid-fast". In the generate condition they saw the cue and only the first letter of the answer, "rapid-f", and had to produce a word that fitted the rule, in this case a synonym. Later, people remembered the words they had produced better than the words they had only read.

The authors tested this with recognition, free recall and cued recall, and with five generation rules: associate, category, opposite, synonym and rhyme. In every case the generate condition came out ahead, and the size of the advantage did not differ significantly between rules. In the cued recall experiment, for example, participants recalled 68 percent of generated responses against 61 percent of read ones. The experiments were small, with 12 to 24 participants each. The authors concluded the effect was real and that it posed an interpretive problem; they could not settle on why it happened.

This is now called the generation effect: information you produce from your own memory tends to be better retained than the same information presented to you.

## Why it might change a decision

The effect makes a checkable prediction about any learning material that can be presented either complete or partly withheld. If two groups study identical content, one reading full statements and one filling in a missing word or answer they can work out, the second group should remember more of those words at a later test. The meta-analyses below add sharper predictions: the gap should be larger for short lists than long ones, and should disappear if the thing to be produced is meaningless, such as a nonword.

## How solid is this?

The generation effect is one of the better replicated findings in memory research, and it has been pooled in at least two large meta-analyses. What they show is a reliable effect of moderate size that depends a good deal on the conditions.

Sharon Bertsch, Bryan Pesta, Richard Wiscott and Michael McDaniel, writing in *Memory & Cognition* in 2007, combined 445 effect sizes from 86 studies with 17,711 participants. The overall effect size was d = 0.40. In plain terms, generated items were remembered at an average rate of .559 against .471 for read items, a gap of about nine percentage points. The variation around that figure was large. The effect was bigger in cued recall (d = 0.55) and recognition (0.46) than in free recall (0.32). It was larger in within-subjects designs (0.50), where the same person both reads and generates, than between subjects (0.28). Lists of 25 items or fewer gave d = 0.60; lists of more than 50 gave 0.09. Tests given more than a day later showed a larger effect (0.64), though that estimate rests on only 30 effect sizes. For nonwords the effect was almost absent, at d = 0.05.

Matthew McCurdy and colleagues revisited the literature in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* in 2020, covering 126 articles and 310 experiments. They estimated that generated items were remembered about 10 percentage points better, or about 8 points after adjusting for possible publication bias, which still left a significant effect. They again found the advantage for words but not for nonwords, and again found it smallest in free recall. They also found it in between-subjects designs, which counts against the idea that it is purely an artefact of people rehearsing generated items at the expense of read ones in a mixed list. A further moderator they emphasised was generation constraint: how tightly the cue limits what can be produced. Less constrained tasks gave better recall, although this difference did not appear for recognition.

The nonword result is the most telling boundary. It fits the view that generation helps by activating what a person already knows about a word's meaning; when there is nothing to activate, there is nothing to gain. Competing accounts, such as greater mental effort or extra attention, have mixed support, and McCurdy and colleagues found that harder and easier generation tasks gave similar effects, which counts against effort alone.

The popular version of the idea, that producing answers is a powerful general study technique, outruns these data. In the 2007 review, most effect sizes came from lists of words, almost all from younger adults, and tests within a day far outnumbered tests after longer delays. The effect is real in that setting; how far it carries to complex material and real courses is a separate question these reviews do not answer.
