## What happened

On Monday 23 November 2026, the Government of Barbados and the United Nations co-hosted a Special Summit on AI Safety in Bridgetown, chaired by Prime Minister Mia Mottley and UN Secretary-General António Guterres. UN News reported about 150 participants, in person and online, including ministers, AI company leaders, heads of AI safety institutes and members of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI. Military uses of AI were left off the agenda.

In his speech, Guterres called on member states to begin discussions on a multilateral AI Risk Management Framework. According to UN News, the elements he listed were shared safety measures, common approaches to testing, clear safeguards and standards, basic transparency requirements, independent evaluation and oversight of advanced models, and support so every country can apply them. He also proposed a Global Fund for AI Capacity-Building and repeated his support for banning autonomous weapons.

In a UN News preview published on 20 November, the UN's envoy on technology, Amandeep Singh Gill, said of the framework: "It's not a treaty, it's not regulation." UN News said no negotiated outcome was planned. The organisers' website lists a Bridgetown Call to Action on AI Safety for Humanity and a discussion paper among the summit documents.

In a conversation with the moderator, Mottley said yes when asked whether her government should have a kill switch to cut off access to AI models in an emergency, and said a developer should be liable if an AI agent hacked a Barbados bank. French President Emmanuel Macron, in a video message, said the Paris Call for AI Security had been signed by 18 heads of state or government and 11 AI leaders, and argued that accepting its principles should be a condition of market access.

The day before, the scientific panel released a thematic brief on capacity-building. UN News reported that it stresses countries testing AI systems in their own languages; panel member Vukosi Marivate of the University of Pretoria said models still perform far worse in many of them.

## What is claimed vs. what is verified

The speeches and their wording are documented by UN News, the UN's own news service. Those are records of what was said, not evidence that the proposals will be taken up.

Some numbers need care. The count of Paris Call signatories differs between UN News articles: a preview quoting Gill gave 17 states and 10 developers, while the report of Macron's message gave 18 and 11. The gap may reflect new signatures, but neither article says so.

Gill cited the World Bank's World Development Report 2026 for a contrast between about 500 incident response teams in high-income countries and six in low-income ones. Tech Times, reporting independently, traced the six to a World Bank count of low-income countries with teams in an international incident response forum, and pointed out that it measures cybersecurity capacity rather than anything specific to AI.

Xinhua, as republished by the Kazakh outlet El.kz, reported that Guterres wanted the framework developed within a year and described contents of the call to action. Those details do not appear in the UN's own account and could not be confirmed here.

## Why it matters

Most AI safety testing capacity sits in a handful of wealthy countries and companies. The proposal would, if pursued, give UN member states a forum to agree on common testing and transparency expectations and a fund to help others take part. It would not, by itself, oblige any developer or government to do anything.

## Open questions and other views

Supporters, including the co-hosts, argue that smaller states are exposed to AI risks they did not create and have no say over, and that a shared baseline is a precondition for later rules. Microsoft's Brad Smith, speaking at the summit, said AI is moving faster than safety work but argued that its spread in the Global South also needs to speed up.

Sceptics question whether pledges will lead anywhere. Tech Times cited an opinion column warning that non-binding commitments can become diplomatic cover, and an analysis by the Council on Foreign Relations noting that four of the scientific panel's 40 experts work mainly at large US technology firms. It also noted that neither the United States nor China has endorsed the Paris Call, and quoted a September statement by President Trump rejecting what he called a globalist scheme. Whether a framework can matter without the countries where most frontier models are built remains open.
