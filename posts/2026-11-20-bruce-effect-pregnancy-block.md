## The idea

In July 1959 Hilda Bruce, working at the National Institute for Medical Research in Mill Hill, London, published a one-page note in *Nature* titled "An exteroceptive block to pregnancy in the mouse". Exteroceptive means triggered by something sensed from outside the body. Her finding was that a female mouse that has just mated with one male can lose that pregnancy if she is then housed with a different, unfamiliar male.

In her setup, a strange male was introduced about 24 hours after mating. Many of those females came back into oestrus and mated again within three to six days, so the first pregnancy had failed. As a comparison, she gave a figure of about 8 percent for young females that return to oestrus that quickly when simply removed from the male after mating. A follow-up paper in the *Journal of Reproduction and Fertility* in 1960 described the block as caused by the mere proximity of strange males, without mating. The phenomenon took her name: the Bruce effect.

The male does not have to be very different. In 1986 Yamazaki and colleagues reported in *PNAS* that block was more frequent when the stud male and the strange male differed only by a mutation in H-2K, one gene of the mouse major histocompatibility complex, than when the two males were genetically identical. The female appears to register the individual, not just the presence of a male.

## Why it might change a decision

The standard evolutionary reading is that a female who is likely to lose her offspring to a newly arrived male (because infanticide by incoming males is common in some species) does better to end a pregnancy early than to carry it to term. That reading makes a specific, checkable prediction: pregnancy loss should rise after male turnover mainly where new males kill infants, and should be low where the previous male stays to defend the female.

For anyone who manages breeding animals, the finding implies something concrete to test in their own records. A mouse colony that keeps recently mated females near unfamiliar males should show lower litter rates than one that keeps them apart; a zoo or reserve that replaces a breeding male in a primate group might see a dip in births in the following months. Both are matters of counting, not belief.

## How solid is this?

In laboratory mice the effect has been reproduced in later work, including Bruce's own 1960 paper and the 1986 genetic study, and the lab evidence is the strongest part of the case. The open question for decades was whether it happens in the wild at all. Edward Heske and Randy Nelson's 1984 paper on prairie voles put the doubt in its title, asking whether pregnancy interruption was a laboratory artefact or a field phenomenon. Field tests in rodents have since been mixed. A 2017 study by Eccard, Dammhahn and Ylönen in *Oecologia* ran three outdoor enclosure experiments with bank voles plus a captive experiment with 205 females, and found delayed births after male turnover mostly in groups of one male and one female. The authors read this as a female adaptation that pays off only at low population density, a narrower claim than the classic lab result.

The most cited field evidence comes from primates. Eila Roberts, Amy Lu, Thore Bergman and Jacinta Beehner reported in *Science* in 2012 on wild geladas in the Simien Mountains of Ethiopia, followed for five years: 110 females in 21 groups, through 28 replacements of the dominant male. Pregnancy was tracked with hormones measured from faecal samples. Females terminated 80 percent of pregnancies in the weeks after a takeover; in the university's account, 8 of 10 females confirmed pregnant at a replacement miscarried. The same release says nearly 44 percent of infants were lost to infanticide in the months after a replacement, which is the cost the theory says termination avoids.

The core gelada number therefore rests on about ten confirmed pregnancies in one population, and it has not been replicated in another wild primate population. The strongest competing explanation is stress: a takeover is a violent social upheaval, and pregnancy loss could be a side effect of that disruption rather than a female strategy. The two are hard to separate in an observational study, since takeovers cannot be assigned at random. How widespread the effect is across mammals, and whether it occurs in humans, is not established by any of the studies above.
