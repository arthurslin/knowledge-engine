## The idea

After a very large earthquake, the whole Earth rings like a struck bell. It vibrates at a set of fixed frequencies, called its normal modes, with periods of minutes, and the ringing fades over days. Seismologists have measured these modes since the 1960s and use them to infer the planet's internal structure. The assumption was that between big earthquakes the bell falls silent.

In March 1998, Naoki Suda, Kazunari Nawa and Yoshio Fukao reported in Science that it does not. They examined ten years of records, from 1986 to 1995, from a sensitive gravimeter at a station in South Africa, cutting them into overlapping three-day windows. Across the whole decade, faint peaks sat at the frequencies of Earth's fundamental spheroidal modes, roughly 2 to 7 millihertz, or periods between about two and a half and eight minutes. The peaks were there on quiet days and were not disturbed by large earthquakes. When the authors stacked 437 quiet days, the peaks stood out clearly.

The obvious explanation was that countless small earthquakes keep the modes topped up. The authors tested this by building synthetic records from an earthquake catalogue plus about 2,000 extra moderate events a year, extrapolated from the usual statistics of earthquake sizes. The synthetic records reproduced the ringing after big quakes but not the continuous background. The authors concluded that earthquakes cannot account for the oscillations and proposed that something near the surface, probably random pressure changes in the atmosphere, was doing the work. The signal became known as Earth's hum.

A concrete way to picture it: on an ordinary day with no major earthquake anywhere, the ground everywhere is rising and falling by tiny amounts in a few slow, regular rhythms, too faint for any person to sense but steady enough for instruments to pick out after averaging.

## Why it might change a decision

The source question produced a checkable prediction. If ocean waves raised by storms supply the energy, the hum's sources should move with the winter storm season, and its strength should rise and fall with measured wave heights. In 2004 Junkee Rhie and Barbara Romanowicz used seismometer arrays in Japan and California to locate the sources. They placed them mainly in the northern Pacific during northern winter and in the Southern Ocean during southern winter, and estimated the total level as equivalent to a magnitude 5.75 to 6 earthquake, which they said small earthquakes cannot supply. Their proposed mechanism was storm energy turned into long, slow ocean waves, called infragravity waves, that press on the seafloor.

The finding also changes what a seismologist can do in a region with few earthquakes. In 2009 Kiwamu Nishida, Jean-Paul Montagner and Hitoshi Kawakatsu took hum records from 54 stations, cross-correlated the signals between pairs of stations, and used the result for seismic tomography of the upper mantle. Their model agreed with maps built from earthquake waves. The specific, testable behaviour is this: a map of deep structure can be built from background vibration alone, and its reliability can be checked against earthquake-based maps where both exist.

## How solid is this?

The existence of the hum is well established. It was first reported in a single study of one station's records, but it has since been detected and used by several independent groups with different instruments and networks. Physics Today's 2005 summary describes it as observed continuously, with amplitudes that, citing work by Göran Ekström, rise around January and July.

The source is less settled. Suda and colleagues favoured the atmosphere and flagged two weaknesses in their own work: noise added inside the instrument before digitising might contribute, and slow earthquakes at ridges and trenches that leave no ordinary seismic signature could not be ruled out. Rhie and Romanowicz shifted the leading view to the oceans. In 2007 Spahr Webb argued in Nature that atmospheric turbulence is far too weak a source and that interacting ocean waves over shallow continental shelves drive the hum, rather than waves in the deep ocean. Physics Today quoted Fukao cautioning that ocean source locations do not by themselves exclude atmospheric pressure acting on the sea surface, and described the mechanism as still being worked out.

So the popular shorthand, "storms make the Earth hum", is broadly consistent with the evidence, but it compresses a real disagreement about where in the ocean, and by what physics, the energy is passed into the solid Earth. The amplitudes involved are tiny: Suda and colleagues put the level at around a nanogal, roughly a trillionth of the pull of gravity at the surface, and every measurement depends on long averaging.
