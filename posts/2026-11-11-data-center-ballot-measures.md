## What happened

On 3 November 2026, voters in local elections across the United States decided dozens of ballot measures about data centres, the large computing facilities whose construction has accelerated with demand for AI. On 6 November the nonpartisan election site Ballotpedia reported that voters had favoured limiting data centre development in 40 of the 41 local contests it tracked.

Ballotpedia's breakdown: 27 approved measures that limited data centres or expressed opposition, and 13 defeated measures that would have supported them. The approved group includes five full bans (one each in California and Florida, three in Ohio), twelve partial bans, three six-month moratoriums in Maine, two Ohio county zoning measures, two nonbinding opposition votes in Illinois, two voter-approval requirements and one financial-guarantee rule. The single exception was Harwood, North Dakota, where voters defeated a proposed review requirement covering data centres. Three more measures, in Arkansas and North Dakota, are due in December.

Ohio dominated. StockAnalysis.com, drawing on Fortune and local outlets, reported that Ohio held 21 of the 41 measures, with about ten towns approving bans, most of them applying only to facilities above 25 megawatts. Among the reported margins: about 55 percent in Grove City for a rule requiring public votes on large projects, more than 87 percent in Perry for requiring financial guarantees from developers seeking tax breaks, and about 80 percent in Trenton for a ban. In Janesville, Wisconsin, voters approved a requirement that any project above 450 million dollars at the former General Motors site go to a popular vote, according to the law firm Venable.

## What is claimed vs. what is verified

The headline count is a tally by one organisation, built from unofficial returns. Ballotpedia does not say the data centre results are certified, and StockAnalysis notes that Ohio results remain unofficial until county boards finish canvassing. Ballotpedia gives no vote shares; the margins above come from news reports and should be read as preliminary.

The count also depends on definitions. "Favoured limits" includes rejecting a pro-development advisory question, which changes nothing on the ground, alongside binding bans. Other outlets used different totals on election night: StockAnalysis cites a Stateline tally of 33 limiting measures passed or leading. Venable, writing independently on 4 November, also counted 41 measures in 10 states and described the results as almost unanimously against development. Sources disagree on how to describe at least one measure, in Ashville, Ohio, where construction had already begun.

Turnout, the size of the projects affected and campaign spending are not systematically reported. In Grove City, pre-election coverage by ABC6 described a citizens' group backing the measure and a separate group, with the local chamber of commerce and the city council, opposing it.

## Why it matters

Several measures directly constrain projects that were planned or underway. StockAnalysis reports that Trenton's ban puts a proposed 250-megawatt industrial park project in question, and that Sunbury's targeted a possible large Amazon facility. Voter-approval rules such as Grove City's and Janesville's add a step to future projects rather than blocking them outright. Because data centre siting is mostly decided locally, a pattern of such rules could shift where AI computing capacity gets built.

## Open questions and other views

Supporters of the measures, such as the Protect Grove City campaign, argue residents should have a say over projects that draw heavily on power, water and land. Opponents, including the Save Grove City campaign and local business groups, argue the rules create uncertainty for growth and can catch other large developments, such as schools and hospitals. Developers and supporters of individual projects have pointed to tax revenue, jobs and investment.

Several questions are unresolved. It is not clear whether the votes reflect AI specifically or broader concerns about electricity bills, water and land use that would apply to any heavy industry. It is not yet known how the new rules will be applied, or whether any will face legal challenges. And 41 contests in mostly small towns may say little about national opinion, since places with active opposition are the ones most likely to put a question on the ballot.
