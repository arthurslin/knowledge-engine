## The idea

Sex is expensive. In a species with separate males and females, an asexual female produces only daughters, each of which can bear young, while a sexual female spends half her output on sons. All else being equal, an asexual lineage should double its share every generation. Yet sexual reproduction is the norm among animals and plants. Explaining why is one of the long-standing puzzles of evolutionary biology.

One family of answers borrows its name from Leigh Van Valen. In 1973, after several journals rejected the paper, he founded a journal, *Evolutionary Theory*, to publish "A new evolutionary law", which argued from the fossil record that a group's chance of extinction stays roughly constant however long it has existed. He called this the Red Queen, after the character in *Through the Looking-Glass* who must keep running to stay in the same place: each species' environment, made up largely of other evolving species, keeps getting worse.

W. D. Hamilton, in a 1980 paper in *Oikos*, applied this logic to sex. He suggested that parasites, which are short-lived and evolve fast relative to their hosts, could favour sex if they are virulent against specific host genotypes. In his models parasites keep adapting to whatever host type is common, producing cycles, and under some of these cycling conditions sexual populations achieve higher long-run fitness than asexual competitors. Recombination helps because it keeps producing rare gene combinations that the parasites are not yet adapted to.

A concrete example comes from *Potamopyrgus antipodarum*, a New Zealand freshwater snail that has both sexual and asexual forms. In a 1987 *Nature* paper, Curtis Lively measured how common males were across populations as a proxy for how much sex was going on. The pattern did not fit the idea that sex is maintained by a variable physical environment, but it did fit selection from parasites.

## Why it might change a decision

The hypothesis makes a time-lagged prediction that can be checked: an asexual clone should do well while it is rare, become the parasites' main target once it is common, and then decline. Sexual hosts should not show the same boom and bust. Jokela, Dybdahl and Lively tracked a mixed population of sexual and asexual snails in New Zealand for ten years from 1994; the details here come from the university's summary of the paper, as the full text could not be checked. Clones that were common at the start became more susceptible to parasites over time, and as infections rose they declined sharply, some disappearing entirely. The sexual snails stayed comparatively stable. For anyone designing a study, the idea says to look for shifting, genotype-specific infection over time, not a fixed advantage of sex at a single moment.

## How solid is this?

The evidence combines theory, field surveys and one well-known laboratory experiment, but it comes from a small number of study systems.

The strongest experimental test is by Levi Morran, Olivia Schmidt, Ian Gelarden, Raymond Parrish and Curtis Lively, published in *Science* in 2011. The nematode worm *Caenorhabditis elegans* normally reproduces by self-fertilisation but can also mate with males. The team let worm populations evolve alongside the bacterial pathogen *Serratia marcescens*. Populations able to both self and outcross ended up outcrossing much more often when the pathogen was coevolving with them. Populations genetically restricted to selfing were driven extinct quickly, while those restricted to outcrossing persisted as host and pathogen kept adapting to each other. This is direct, controlled evidence that a coevolving parasite can favour mixing genes from two parents, at least in this pairing. It does not show that parasites are why sex arose, and lab conditions are simpler than the wild.

The field studies are correlational. Snail populations differ in many ways besides parasite load, so a link between male frequency and parasites is consistent with the Red Queen without proving it.

The main competing explanation is about harmful mutations. Alexey Kondrashov argued in *Nature* in 1988 that if deleterious mutations arise at more than about one per genome per generation, sexual populations can purge them more efficiently than asexual ones. On this view sex is a cleaning mechanism, not an arms race. Other proposals emphasise changing physical environments, which Lively's snail data did not support. The two ideas are not mutually exclusive, and how much each contributes in a given species is unresolved. The popular version, that parasites are the reason sex exists, goes further than the evidence; the narrower claim, that coevolving parasites can select for sex in real organisms, has good support.
