## The idea

Give the bottom of a food chain more to eat and you might expect everything above it to do better. In 1971 the ecologist Michael Rosenzweig, then at the State University of New York at Albany, published a short paper in *Science* arguing that, in a common class of models, the opposite can happen. Working through six models of a predator and its prey, he found that increasing the supply of the prey's limiting nutrient or energy tends to destroy the steady state. He called it the paradox of enrichment.

The mechanism comes from how predators feed. In these models a predator's intake rises with prey density but levels off, because catching and handling each prey item takes time (a "type II" functional response). With little food around, the prey's own limits keep everyone in check and the two populations settle at an equilibrium. Raise the prey's carrying capacity enough and that equilibrium becomes unstable: prey surge, predators boom on them, prey crash, predators starve, and the cycle repeats as a limit cycle. Raise it further and the troughs get so deep that one species can blink out by chance.

For decades this was mostly a result on paper. The cleanest test came in 2000, when Gregor Fussmann, Stephen Ellner, Kyle Shertzer and Nelson Hairston at Cornell grew a tiny planktonic predator, the rotifer *Brachionus calyciflorus*, with its food, the green alga *Chlorella vulgaris*, in 380-millilitre glass chemostats: flasks that continuously drip in fresh medium and wash out an equal volume. Nitrogen limited the algae. The team could turn two dials: how much nitrogen came in, and how fast the flask was flushed (the dilution rate).

At low nitrogen the system did what a four-equation model of it predicted. At a very low dilution rate both species sat steady; at intermediate rates they cycled; at high rates they were steady again. That is two transitions between calm and cycling, known as Hopf bifurcations, and the experiment located them roughly where the model said they would be. At high nitrogen, in four trials, the rotifers grazed the algae down to low density and then went extinct. Enrichment, in other words, did not make the predator safer.

## Why it might change a decision

The idea makes a specific prediction that can be checked: in a predator-prey system where predators feed with a saturating response and the prey has no refuge, adding nutrients should increase the amplitude of population swings rather than simply raising average numbers. Someone managing a fish pond, a bioreactor or a nutrient-loaded lake could test this by enriching some replicate units and not others and comparing how much populations vary over time, not just their mean size. The theory also says where to look for exceptions: if the prey includes inedible or defended types, or predators interfere with each other, the destabilising effect should shrink or vanish.

## How solid is this?

The experimental support rests mainly on laboratory systems, and the most cited test is a single study. Fussmann and colleagues ran 18 chemostat trials with one predator and one prey species. Their model matched the qualitative pattern well, but they report that its predicted cycle periods were too long and some quantitative details were off. Earlier laboratory cultures, such as Luckinbill's 1973 work with protists, also produced cycles, which the 2000 paper cites.

Outside the lab the picture is weaker. A 2007 review by Shovonlal Roy and Joydev Chattopadhyay in the *Journal of Biosciences* notes that destabilisation due to enrichment has rarely been observed in real ecosystems, and lists experiments that supported it alongside others that did not. It asks directly why the paradox is so rarely, if ever, detected in nature.

The strongest competing explanation is that real food webs break the model's assumptions. Proposed fixes include inedible or defended prey that soak up nutrients without feeding predators, spatial structure, and predators whose intake depends on the ratio of prey to predators rather than on prey density alone. Under ratio-dependent feeding, the instability largely disappears; whether that form describes real predators is itself an unresolved argument. A popular telling that "pollution makes ecosystems collapse because of the paradox of enrichment" therefore outruns the data: the mechanism is real in controlled conditions, while its importance in lakes and fields is not established.
