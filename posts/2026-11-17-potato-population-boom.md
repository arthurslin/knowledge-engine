## The idea

Between 1700 and 1900 the population of Europe, Asia and Africa grew far faster than in previous centuries. Many explanations compete: better medicine, trade, new farming methods, colonial wealth. Economists Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian proposed putting a number on one candidate, the potato, which arrived from the Americas in the 1500s and was widely grown in the Old World from the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.

The difficulty is that places that ate more potatoes may differ in other ways. Their solution was a natural experiment. Whether a patch of land suits potatoes depends on climate and soil, which existed long before anyone planted them. So they compared 130 Old World countries, asking whether countries with more potato-suitable land grew faster after the crop's adoption than before it, relative to countries with less. This is a difference-in-differences design: each country is compared with itself over time, and the change is compared across countries.

Their answer, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2011, was that the potato accounts for about 25-26 percent of the increase in Old World population between 1700 and 1900, and 27-34 percent of the increase in urbanisation. The lower numbers come from their most conservative specifications.

One concrete check sits underneath the cross-country result. Using records of French soldiers born in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, they report that men from villages with land fully suitable for potatoes were roughly half an inch taller after the crop's arrival, which is what you would expect if the potato improved childhood nutrition rather than only allowing more people to be fed.

A follow-up by C. Justin Cook (2014) added a twist. He found the potato's estimated effect was larger in populations with high lactase persistence, the ability to digest milk as an adult. Potatoes are rich in calories and vitamin C but low in some nutrients that milk supplies. Where lactase persistence approaches one, he reports the potato's effect more than doubles relative to Nunn and Qian's baseline.

## Why it might change a decision

The finding is mostly about the past, but it bears on how to read any claim that a single innovation explains a share of growth. A specific habit it supports: when you see such a figure, check whether it holds when comparing places within the same region, not only across the world. For the potato, as described below, the estimate falls from about a quarter to about a sixth when countries are compared only with neighbours on the same continent. If an estimate disappears under that check, it was probably measuring regional differences rather than the innovation.

## How solid is this?

The core evidence is one observational study with a careful design, extended by a second study using the same data. It is not an experiment, and the cross-country part relies on rough historical population estimates and modern measures of land suitability, from FAO crop models, projected back centuries.

The authors run many robustness checks. The effect survives controls for other New World staples such as maize, cassava and sweet potato; for legal origin, colonial history, Atlantic trade, slave exports and distance from the coast; and for comparisons within continents and between cities within the same country. They also report no effect in earlier periods before the crop's adoption, which is what you would expect if the result were not just a long-running trend.

The strongest competing explanation is the one the authors themselves name as the greatest threat: Western Europe has a lot of potato-suitable land and also diverged economically for many unrelated reasons, from institutions to coal. If those causes are not fully absorbed by the controls, the potato gets credit it did not earn.

An independent critical review by Pablo Villalobos and Jaime Sevilla, commissioned by the Forethought Foundation, re-ran the analysis. They reproduced the main estimate and found the overall effect still amounted to around a quarter of growth. Restricting comparisons to within continents shrank it to about one-sixth, and adding continental controls cut it by about a third. They also flagged strong spatial autocorrelation, meaning neighbouring countries look alike, which can make results look more certain than they are. Their bottom line: the effect is real but probably somewhere between one-sixth and one-quarter, and the data cannot tell whether the potato raised the long-run growth rate or gave a one-off boost to the population level.

So the popular framing, that the potato "caused" a quarter of the boom, is at the top of the range the evidence supports rather than its centre. That still makes it one of the larger single contributions estimated for any crop.
