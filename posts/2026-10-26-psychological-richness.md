Ask a psychologist what makes a life good and you'll usually get one of two answers. The hedonic answer: feeling good — pleasure, satisfaction, the absence of suffering. The eudaimonic answer: doing good — purpose, contribution, a life that adds up to something. These two have structured the field for decades.

Shigehiro Oishi and Erin Westgate argue a third thing was quietly left out. They call it **psychological richness**: a life characterised by variety, interest, and experiences that change how you see things.

Their argument for why it was missed is structural rather than philosophical. Existing well-being measures map neatly onto four of the Big Five personality traits — extraversion, emotional stability, conscientiousness, agreeableness. Openness to experience had no corresponding dimension. There was a hole in the grid, and richness is what fits it.

## The part that's genuinely strange

Rich experiences don't have to be pleasant. They don't have to be purposeful either.

Westgate's lab has tracked students through approaching hurricanes, sampling their emotions as the storms closed in. Many rated the experience as psychologically rich — it changed how they saw the world — while plainly not enjoying it and while it served no purpose they'd chosen.

That decoupling is the whole idea. It implies something uncomfortable about the other two dimensions: a happy, meaningful life can be monotonous. A steady job, a stable family, work that matters — genuinely good by both conventional measures, and possibly very repetitive. The framework gives you a vocabulary for a dissatisfaction that otherwise sounds like ingratitude.

## What it might change

It adds a third question to decisions that currently get two. Not just *will this make me happy* and *does this matter*, but *will this change how I see things*.

A related finding gives that a testable edge. Aaron Heller and colleagues combined GPS tracking with experience sampling and found that days with more variability in physical location were days with higher positive affect. The obvious experiment: deliberately vary where you spend time for a fortnight, log your mood, and see whether the pattern holds for you specifically. Individual results are the only ones you actually have access to.

## How solid is this?

Mixed. It's worth being precise, because this is the kind of idea that travels faster than its evidence.

**The construct is new and partly contested.** Richness correlates moderately with established eudaimonic measures — self-acceptance at r = .54, purpose in life at r = .47. Related but not identical, which is the authors' point. Whether that gap represents a genuinely distinct dimension or a repackaging of openness is a live argument, not a settled one.

**The Heller finding is correlational, and the authors say so.** They describe the location–affect relationship as bidirectional. Feeling good is itself a reason to leave the house. Nobody has shown that engineering more variety into your week causes the mood improvement, and the study wasn't designed to. The neuroimaging arm rested on 58 participants, small for individual-differences brain work.

**Independent prediction is weak where it's been tested.** A study of 2,644 Facebook users attempted to predict all three well-being dimensions from language. Psychological richness came last, at r = .21 — below both hedonic and eudaimonic well-being.

**One correlate, reported neutrally.** In Oishi and Westgate's samples, happy and meaningful lives were associated with conservative worldviews, while psychologically rich lives were not. That is an observed association in particular samples. It is not evidence that any political orientation produces a better life, and I'd read nothing prescriptive into it in either direction.

The honest summary: an interesting construct with suggestive cross-sectional support, no causal evidence that pursuing it works, and open questions about whether it's distinct enough to need its own name.

## Questions for you

1. What distinguishes psychological richness from happiness and meaning — and why does the hurricane finding matter to that distinction?
2. Which Big Five trait does the construct exist to cover, and what does that say about where the idea came from?
3. Heller et al. found location variety and good mood move together. What's the most defensible reading of that, and what would you need to see to claim causation?
4. Think of a choice you're currently weighing. Does the third question change your answer, or just your description of it?
5. Suppose the construct turns out to be openness to experience wearing a hat. Would anything in the practical advice change?
