## The idea

Walk through a gallery of European portraits and count which cheek each sitter turns toward you. The left cheek wins more often than the right. The pattern was described in formal portraits from the 16th to 20th centuries by McManus and Humphrey, and in 1973 M. Labar reported in *Nature* that the same leftward tendency showed up in ordinary 1972 school yearbook photographs, with no clear difference between boys and girls.

The most influential explanation came from M. E. Nicholls and colleagues at the University of Melbourne in 1999. Their argument starts from hemispheric lateralisation: the right half of the brain is thought to play a larger part in producing emotional expression, and it mainly controls the left side of the face. If the left side of the face is the more expressive one, a sitter who wants to look warm might turn it toward the painter, often without knowing why.

Nicholls's team tested competing explanations. If the bias came from painters' handedness, left-handed artists should show it less; they did not. Then they asked volunteers to pose. Told to show as much emotion as possible for a family portrait, people tended to turn their left cheek to the camera. Told to pose as scientists keeping emotion out of it, they tended to turn the right. To match, the leftward bias was absent in portraits of Royal Society scientists and weaker in portraits of men.

## Why it might change a decision

The claim makes a specific, checkable prediction about photographs. A headshot with the left cheek forward should, on average, be rated as more emotionally expressive, and one with the right cheek forward as more composed. Anyone choosing a profile photo or a book-jacket portrait could test this directly: take both versions, mirror neither, and ask people who do not know the hypothesis to rate them. If the hypothesis is right, the ratings should differ in that direction more often than not; if the ratings show no pattern, that is evidence against it for this kind of image.

## How solid is this?

The basic tendency has been found repeatedly, but not everywhere, and its explanation is contested. Peter White of Cardiff University, writing in *Laterality* in 2019, notes that earlier studies are mixed: some report a left bias, some a right bias, some none.

White's own study is the largest test of how the bias behaves over time. He coded 897 single-figure portraits painted before 1900 from the full collections of the Louvre, a Florentine collection, London's National Gallery and the Prado, then checked the results against a second sample of 2,287 paintings from the National Portrait Gallery in London. In the first sample, 480 portraits turned left and 324 turned right, a clear overall lean (the remaining 93 were classed as neither). The lean was stronger for women (about 71 percent left-oriented) than for men (about 55 percent).

The time trend is the striking part. About 70 percent of portraits before 1600 were left-oriented. In the 1600s and 1700s the bias disappeared, and male sitters in the 1600s actually leaned right. It returned in the 1800s and 1900s. The two samples tracked each other across overlapping periods, which makes a quirk of one collection less likely. White also reports that Italian painters were more strongly left-leaning (69 percent) than others (54 percent), a comparison he labels post hoc and only suggestive.

That pattern is a problem for a purely biological story. Brain organisation did not change between 1550 and 1650, yet the bias did. White's reading is that cultural and art-historical factors, such as the conventions of early Italian portraiture, likely shape the bias, possibly on top of a smaller biological push. He is explicit that historical interpretation of this kind cannot be tested experimentally, and that gallery collections reflect what survived and what curators bought.

Several gaps remain. The posing experiments involve volunteers imagining a portrait, not sitters choosing a pose for a real commission. Lighting conventions, which often fall from one side, were not tested as an alternative in White's study. And the popular version of the claim, that the left side of the face is simply "the better side", outruns the data: the effect is a tendency of roughly 60 to 40 in large samples, it varies by century and by sex, and the reason for it is not settled.
