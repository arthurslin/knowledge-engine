## What happened

On 17 November 2026 Gallup published results from a survey of 15,925 people aged 15 to 22 in 16 countries, run with the Boston Children's Digital Wellness Lab and supported by the Pinterest Impact Fund. A full report, *Friendship in an Online-Offline World*, and a press release followed on 18 November. Fieldwork ran from 28 May to 21 August 2026.

The study is mainly about friendship in person and online, but it includes a section on AI. According to the report, a median of 33 percent of respondents across the countries said they had used AI as a social companion or friend. Larger shares had used AI for other things: a median of 82 percent to ask a question, and about two thirds (68 percent) for advice.

Companionship use rose with general AI use. Half of those who use AI at least daily reported it, compared with 35 percent of weekly users and 24 percent of less frequent users. It was also more common among people who already had an online-only friend: 42 percent, against 26 percent of those without one (a chart note suggests this comparison is among people who had used AI in the past month).

Among AI companion users, ratings of quality varied widely. In Australia 45 percent rated their AI companionship as high quality, compared with 35 percent in Kenya and South Africa and 12 percent in Poland.

The finding that drew most attention concerns loneliness. The report says young people who use AI as a companion had 70 percent higher adjusted odds of being in the higher-loneliness group, after accounting for friendship quality, satisfaction with social time and other characteristics. Loneliness was measured with a standard three-item scale.

## What is claimed vs. what is verified

The figures above are Gallup's own survey estimates; I checked them against the report text. Independent coverage, including 404 Media and Digital Trends, reported the same headline number but did not reanalyse the data, and neither quoted outside researchers. Some details in that coverage drift from the report: 404 Media gave the sample as more than 16,000 and described the survey as online only, while the report lists 15,925 respondents and face-to-face interviews in Kenya and South Africa. Digital Trends gave a US companionship figure of 26 percent; the 26 percent in the report is the comparison group without an online-only friend, and US country figures appear only in charts.

Several limits are stated by Gallup itself. In 14 countries the samples were non-probability web panels, weighted to improve representativeness; only Kenya and South Africa used probability samples. Gallup notes its error estimates do not account for possible coverage and selection biases. The report gives the 70 percent figure without a confidence interval or the full list of adjustment variables, and it does not print the exact wording of the companionship question. The report also says its quality ratings for AI should be compared cautiously with those for human friendships.

## Why it matters

This is one of the larger cross-national attempts to measure how often young people treat AI as a social presence rather than a tool, with samples from six continents. It gives regulators, schools and developers a baseline figure that is not drawn only from the United States or from one app's users. Because companionship use is concentrated among the heaviest AI users and those with online-only friendships, the overall median may hide groups for whom it is far more common.

## Open questions and other views

The central question is direction. The report says the findings cannot establish whether AI companionship supplements, substitutes for or otherwise influences human relationships, and cannot determine whether young people turn to AI because they feel lonely. One reading, voiced in some coverage, is that AI companions may draw time and effort away from human friendships. Another, equally consistent with the data, is that already lonely young people seek out AI, in which case the association says more about need than about harm. A third possibility is that unmeasured factors drive both.

Measurement is a second question. Without the question wording, it is hard to know whether "companion" means a sustained relationship or an occasional chat about feelings, and the answer could vary by language. A general concern with web panels is that they can over-represent heavy internet users, which could push AI figures up; the two face-to-face countries offer a partial check.

A longitudinal design that follows the same young people over time, or one that records actual app use rather than self-report, would be needed to move beyond association.
