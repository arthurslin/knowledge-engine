## The idea

Most advice about study materials aims to make them easier to read: clear fonts, good spacing, plain layouts. A 2011 paper in the journal Cognition argued the opposite might sometimes help. Connor Diemand-Yauman, Daniel Oppenheimer and Erikka Vaughan proposed that perceptual disfluency, the small extra effort of reading an awkward font, would push readers to process material more deeply and so remember more of it. The idea fit a wider research theme called desirable difficulty: some kinds of effort during learning, such as testing yourself, improve later memory even though they feel harder at the time.

Their first study was small. According to a summary from the authors' lab and a report in the BPS Research Digest, 28 adults spent 90 seconds learning seven features of each of three fictional alien species. Some read the facts in 16-point Arial; others read them in 12-point Comic Sans or 12-point Bodoni. After 15 minutes of distraction, the clear-font group answered 72.8 percent of questions correctly and the disfluent group 86.5 percent.

A second study moved into a high school in Ohio, with roughly 220 students. Teachers of history, English and science used altered materials, in fonts such as Comic Sans Italic, Haettenschweiler or Monotype Corsiva, in one class and their normal materials in another. On ordinary class assessments, the students given harder-to-read material did better, with a reported effect size of d = 0.45. The authors noted that the finding needed further study and that discouraged students might simply give up.

The result was widely covered in the press, because it suggested a free, easy change to teaching materials.

## Why it might change a decision

The finding makes a concrete prediction. If a teacher or a student reformats the same material into a harder font, and nothing else changes, later test scores should be higher. That is easy to test: randomly assign people to fonts, give everyone the same material and the same test after a delay, and compare. The evidence below comes mostly from studies that did exactly this.

## How solid is this?

The original claim rested on two studies, one with 28 participants and one in a single school where whole classes rather than individual students received each version. Small samples like this can produce large effects by chance, and later work has not supported a general benefit.

A closely related claim, that disfluent fonts make people better at solving trick maths problems by prompting more careful thought, was tested in a 2015 paper in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General by Andrew Meyer, Shane Frederick and colleagues. They pooled data from the original experiment with 16 attempts to replicate it and found no effect on how many problems were solved. The harder fonts slightly increased response times.

For learning, the clearest summary is a 2018 meta-analysis in Educational Psychology Review by Xie, Zhou and Liu. Across 25 articles and 3,135 participants, the pooled effect of disfluent text on recall was d = -0.01 and on transfer d = 0.03, both essentially zero. Two things did change: disfluent readers spent more time studying (d = 0.52) and rated their own learning lower (d = -0.43). In other words, the fonts changed how studying felt, not how much was retained.

That meta-analysis has its own problems. In 2021 Sophia Weissgerber, Matthias Brunmair and Ralf Rummer, in the same journal, reported coding errors, mislabelled results and effect sizes they could not reproduce, and noted that the original data and code were not shared. Correcting one error left the transfer result close to zero, which they described as fairly robust, but they said the recall estimate and moderator analyses were less credible. So the precise pooled numbers should be held loosely, while the broad picture of no reliable benefit is not overturned.

Independent experiments point the same way. In 2020 Andrea Taylor, Kimberley Wade, Maryanne Garry and colleagues tested Sans Forgetica, a font designed specifically to boost memory, across four experiments with 882 people, published in Memory. People rated it harder to read than Arial. On a word-pair task they remembered fewer pairs in Sans Forgetica, and on prose and comprehension tasks scores were nearly identical (74 versus 73 percent; about 37.6 versus 37.1 percent). According to Warwick's press release, the font's developers had reported a benefit only in an unpublished study.

The strongest remaining case for disfluency is that it may help under narrow conditions, such as particular test delays or particular kinds of learners, which some researchers continue to study. The competing explanation for the 2011 results is simpler: small samples, flexible field conditions and chance. The popular version of the claim, that awkward fonts are a reliable study aid, outruns the data.
